About the book

Most people get two things wrong about estate planning: they think it’s only for rich people, and they think protecting what they’ve built has to be complicated, expensive, and terrifying. It doesn’t. If you own a home — or even part of one — if you have kids, if you have a savings account, if there are people who depend on you, then you have an estate. It might be modest. It is still worth protecting.

This book explains living trusts in plain, warm, honest English — zero legalese, every legal term explained in everyday words on first use. It admits where the answer is “it depends, ask an attorney.” And it is fine — encouraged — for the book to help a reader conclude they do not need a trust.

The bottom line

This is the chapter that moves from reading to doing — but don't try to check every box this weekend. Start with Checklist 1, then the next step, then the next. A trust built slowly and thoughtfully beats a rushed one every time.

Keep these pages. Once your plan is in place, Checklist 6 is your standing appointment with yourself: twenty minutes a year, one professional visit every few years, and a quick look whenever life changes.

Key takeaways

  • Work the checklists in order — decide, create, fund, complete, digitize, maintain. Each stage builds on the last, and funding (Checklist 3) gives everything else its power: a trust that owns nothing does nothing.
    1. Repeat the yearly habits. Check account beneficiaries, confirm everything is still in the trust, verify your trustee is still willing and able, revisit your digital-asset inventory — small checks prevent the slow drift that undoes good plans.
    2. Bring these checklists to your first attorney meeting. You'll be the prepared, informed client attorneys hope for — and run anything important past a licensed attorney in your area before you sign, transfer, or retitle anything.

    Plain-English glossary

    Every term in this book, in dictionary order, in the chapters’ own plain words. This glossary explains general ideas in everyday words — it makes you informed, not your own attorney. Details vary by state, so confirm anything important with a licensed attorney in your area.

    Administrator.
    The person a court appoints to settle a deceased person's affairs when no will names an executor — same job, chosen by the court instead of the deceased.
    Agent (under a power of attorney).
    The person you name in a power of attorney to act for you. "Attorney" here doesn't mean lawyer — it means someone you've given authority to, chosen by you.
    Amendment.
    A formal written change to an existing trust — a new successor trustee, an added child. It must be signed properly; margin notes don't count.
    Bar association.
    The organization of licensed attorneys in a state. Most bar websites have a lawyer-referral page — a reliable way to find an estate-planning attorney — plus low-cost legal aid listings.
    Beneficiary.
    The person (or organization) who receives something from a trust, account, or policy — the one the arrangement is meant to benefit.
    Beneficiary designation.
    The form — on a bank account, retirement account, or life insurance policy — naming who gets that asset when you die. The name on this form wins, and the asset bypasses probate entirely.
    Buy-sell agreement.
    A written agreement between business co-owners saying what happens to an owner's share if they die. With one in place, succession planning is largely handled.
    Certification of trust.
    A short summary, prepared by your attorney, proving the trust exists. Banks usually accept it in place of the full document when you retitle accounts into the trust's name.
    Cold wallet.
    Crypto stored on a physical device kept offline — safer from hackers, but it can still be lost.
    Common-law property system.
    The system most states use: what's in your name is generally yours, what's in your spouse's name is theirs. Contrast with community property.
    Community property.
    The system a handful of states use: anything earned or bought during the marriage belongs to both spouses equally, no matter whose name is on the title. Premarital or inherited property usually stays separate.
    Conservator / Conservatorship.
    A court-appointed person who manages the money of someone incapacitated. Without a durable power of attorney, a family member must petition a court — hearings, supervision, and legal costs at the worst possible time.
    Cryptocurrency.
    Digital money — like Bitcoin — that exists only online and is controlled by secret codes. There is no customer service desk: lose the codes, lose the assets.
    Deed.
    The paper saying who owns a piece of real estate. Moving a house into your trust means a new deed — deed retitling — from you to you as trustee, then recording it: filing it with the local property-records office.
    Digital assets.
    Anything you own or have rights to in digital form — cloud photos, email, social media, online accounts, crypto, domain names, loyalty points. Each sits behind a password, and passwords die with their owners unless a plan is made.
    Distribution.
    Handing trust or estate assets out to beneficiaries — the moment your instructions become actual transfers of money or property.
    Due-on-sale clause.
    A line in most mortgage contracts letting the lender demand full repayment if the property changes hands. Confirm with your lender before moving a mortgaged house into your trust.
    Durable power of attorney.
    A power of attorney that stays valid even after you become incapacitated. A non-durable one quits the moment you lose the ability to decide — exactly when you need it most.
    Elective share.
    In many states, the guaranteed minimum share a surviving spouse can claim no matter what the will says — "elective" because the spouse chooses it instead of the will's gift. Size and calculation differ everywhere.
    Estate.
    Everything a person owned when they died — the legal word for "your stuff," mansion or mattress-with-cash alike.
    Executor.
    The person named in a will to carry out its instructions — the estate's project manager: gathering assets, paying final bills, distributing what's left. With no will, the court's equivalent is an administrator.
    Funding (a trust).
    Moving your assets into the trust — changing the ownership label on your house, accounts, and other property so the trust owns them instead of you. The document builds the bucket; funding fills it. An unfunded trust does nothing.
    Grantor.
    The person who creates the trust and puts things into it — "the person who sets it up." Usually you.
    Guardian.
    The adult who raises your minor children if both parents die or can't care for them. You name guardians in your will — a trust cannot do this.
    Healthcare agent.
    The person you name in your healthcare directive to make medical decisions for you when you can't — talking with your doctors and carrying out your wishes. Also called a healthcare proxy; labels vary.
    Healthcare directive (advance directive / living will).
    Your written medical wishes for when you can't speak: your treatment wishes (what care you want or don't want) and your healthcare agent (who speaks for you). It ends the guessing in the hospital waiting room.
    Holographic will.
    A will written entirely in your own handwriting, with no witnesses. Some states recognize these; others don't.
    Hot wallet.
    Crypto stored online or in an app — convenient, but exposed to hackers. Contrast with a cold wallet.
    Incapacity / Incapacitated.
    Being alive but unable to make or communicate sound decisions — after a stroke, serious illness, advanced dementia, or severe injury. A trust's incapacity planning exists for this.
    Intestacy (dying intestate).
    Dying without a will or trust. The state's default inheritance formula — the one-size-fits-all "chef's choice" plan — then decides who gets what.
    Irrevocable trust.
    A trust that generally cannot be changed, taken back, or dissolved once created and funded — the assets are no longer yours. Used for specialist purposes like Medicaid planning or large-estate strategies; never casually, always with an attorney.
    Joint tenancy (with right of survivorship).
    One flavor of joint ownership: when one owner dies, their share passes automatically to the surviving owner — no trust, no probate. Ask your attorney before moving jointly owned property into a trust.
    Legacy contact (and inactive account manager).
    Planning tools the platforms themselves offer: Facebook's legacy contact manages a memorialized profile (but can't read private messages); some services offer an inactive account manager. Your trust can't override a company's terms — these tools can.
    Limited-scope representation.
    When an attorney handles one defined piece of a job — like reviewing a DIY trust for a flat fee — rather than the whole thing. Confirm they offer it before you start the DIY draft.
    Living trust.
    A trust that exists while you're alive — as opposed to one created only after you die. "Living" doesn't mean complicated; it means it starts now. In this book it means a revocable living trust unless stated otherwise.
    Notary.
    A person licensed by the state to officially verify signatures — they check your ID, watch you sign, and stamp the document. Some states require notarization for trust or will signings; either way, it makes a document harder to challenge.
    Operating agreement.
    For an LLC, the document listing who owns the company. Assigning your business interest to your trust typically means updating it to show the trust as owner of your share.
    Password manager (with emergency access).
    A secure app storing all your passwords in one vault behind a single master password. With emergency access, a trusted person you name gets in after a waiting period if you can't respond. Never put passwords in your trust document.
    Payable-on-death (POD).
    A beneficiary form offered by banks: fill in a name, and when you die the account goes straight to that person — no probate, no trust. The investment-account equivalent is transfer-on-death (TOD).
    Pour-over will.
    A short will catching anything left out of your trust — "anything I own that isn't already in my trust goes into my trust at my death." It also names guardians for minor children. But those assets still pass through probate: a safety net, not the plan.
    Power of attorney (financial).
    A document naming an agent to handle your financial affairs — paying bills, managing accounts, filing taxes — for anything the trust doesn't reach, while you're alive but incapacitated. You want the durable version.
    Private key / Seed phrase.
    The secret controlling your crypto — a code, or a list of 12 or 24 ordinary words. Whoever holds it owns the funds: the only key to a safe, with no spare. Write it on paper, stored physically and apart from the instructions saying where it is.
    Probate.
    The court-supervised process of settling a deceased person's affairs — paying debts, then distributing what's left to the legal heirs. Slow, public, never free — but for good reasons: order, fairness, fraud prevention, settling disagreements.
    Restatement.
    A fresh rewrite of your entire trust — same name, same original date, but every page new, with all updates folded in. The better choice when amendments have piled up or your life has changed beyond recognition.
    Revocable trust.
    A trust you can change, update, or cancel entirely while alive and mentally competent — add or remove assets, change terms, or dissolve it. It becomes irrevocable when you die, locking your final wishes in place. This is the trust this book is about.
    Self-proving affidavit.
    A short statement signed in front of a notary confirming your will's witnesses are who they say they are. It makes documents much easier to use later; availability depends on your state.
    Small-estate procedure.
    Every state's shortcut for small estates — an easier, cheaper path than full probate when the estate is under a dollar cutoff (a small-estate affidavit or summary administration). The cutoff varies wildly by state — one reason "do I even need a trust?" has no universal answer.
    Special needs trust.
    A trust holding money for a disabled person without disqualifying them from needs-based government benefits, with a trustee managing the funds for their care. Professional help is genuinely important here.
    Successor trustee.
    The backup you name to take over the trust when you can't — during incapacity, and after death to carry out your instructions. They step in automatically: no court, no permission slip. Name more than one backup, and review the list every few years.
    Tax-neutral.
    What a revocable living trust is for income-tax purposes: nothing changes. While you're alive and in charge, you report the trust's income on your own return as before — no separate tax life.
    Transfer-on-death (TOD).
    The investment-account equivalent of payable-on-death: name a beneficiary on the account, and it passes straight to them at your death, skipping probate.
    Trust agreement (trust document).
    The written instructions that make the trust real — trustee, successor, beneficiaries, what happens to each asset. A trust is only official once this document is properly signed.
    Trustee.
    The person (or company) in charge of managing the property in a trust, following your instructions. While you're alive and well, that's usually you — which is why daily life doesn't change when you create a trust.
    Will.
    Your written instructions for after you die: who gets what, who carries it out (the executor), and — the job only a will can do — who raises your minor children. A will doesn't skip probate; it tells the court what to do inside it.
    Witness.
    Someone who watches you sign a legal document and signs too, confirming "yes, I saw this person sign this." Some places require one witness, some two; the rule is state law.
    1. Legal words are ordinary ideas in fancy dress. A grantor creates, a trustee manages, a beneficiary receives — the terms in this book unpack simply once you hear them in plain English.
    2. Two words do the most work in your whole plan: funding (moving assets into the trust — the step everyone skips) and durable (the word that keeps your power of attorney alive exactly when you need it).
    3. When a term touches your real life, take it to your attorney. Only a licensed attorney in your state can say how these words apply to your family.
